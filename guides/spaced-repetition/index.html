Learn vocabulary with spaced repetition: how it works (FSRS explained)

Spaced repetition means reviewing each word just before you would forget it, with gaps between reviews that grow every time you remember it: a day, a few days, a week, then weeks and months. Combined with testing yourself instead of rereading, it is the most reliable way to make vocabulary stick for the long term.

Most people learn the opposite way: read the list a few times the evening before the test, pass the test, and forget half the words a week later. That isn’t a lack of talent. It’s how memory works when you review at the wrong moments. This guide explains the forgetting curve, why testing yourself beats rereading, what the rating buttons in flashcard apps mean, and what the FSRS algorithm adds.

The forgetting curve: why new words disappear

In the 1880s the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables and measured how much he still knew after different delays. The result is known as the forgetting curve: memory drops fast in the first hours and days after learning, then the decline slows down.

The more useful part of his work is what happens when you review. Each time you successfully recall something, the curve gets flatter, so the next review can wait longer. A word you have recalled several times at growing intervals can stay with you for months between reviews. That is the whole principle behind spaced repetition.

Spaced repetition and active recall

Spaced repetition answers the question “when should I review?”. Active recall answers “how should I review?”. Rereading a list feels productive because the words look familiar, but recognition is much easier than producing the word yourself. Research on the testing effect consistently finds that retrieving an answer from memory strengthens it more than looking at it again.

Flashcards combine both ideas: you see one side, try to remember the other, and only then check. A scheduler then decides when that card comes back.

  • Look at the front and say or type the answer before flipping.
  • Be honest with yourself about whether you really knew it.
  • Let the schedule decide which cards are due instead of reviewing everything every day.
  • Learn in both directions for words you need to produce, not just recognize.

Again, Hard, Good, Easy: what the buttons mean

Many flashcard apps ask you to rate each answer with four buttons. The rating is not a grade for you. It is information the scheduler uses to plan the next review.

What FSRS is

FSRS stands for Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler. It is an open-source scheduling algorithm developed in the spaced repetition community, and Anki added it as an option in 2023. Older schedulers, such as the SM-2 family that many apps have used for decades, mostly multiply an interval by a fixed factor. FSRS instead keeps a small model of your memory for each card.

That model tracks how difficult a card is for you, how stable the memory is (roughly, how long it lasts before you are likely to forget), and the current probability that you can still recall it. After every answer the values are updated, and the next review is scheduled for the point where your recall probability is expected to drop to a target level, commonly around 90 percent. The model’s parameters can be fitted to real review histories, which is why it adapts to how you actually remember rather than to a one-size-fits-all formula.

In practice that means fewer unnecessary reviews of words you already know, and quicker returns for the ones that keep slipping. Cardfox schedules every card with FSRS, so the Again/Hard/Good/Easy answers you give (or a swipe) feed directly into that model.

A daily routine that works

Spaced repetition only pays off if you show up regularly. Short daily sessions beat long weekly ones because the schedule is built around specific days.

  • Review your due cards first, every day. That is the part that keeps old words alive.
  • Add new words in small batches, for example one textbook unit at a time, rather than hundreds at once.
  • Don’t skip Again out of pride. Marking a forgotten word honestly is what makes the schedule accurate.
  • If a word keeps failing, change the card: add an example sentence, an image or a memory aid.
  • Expect more reviews in the first weeks after adding many new cards. The load evens out as intervals grow.

Exams and deadlines

Pure spaced repetition doesn’t know about your test on Friday. If you have a fixed date, start early enough that each word gets several reviews before it, and keep adding new units at a steady pace. In Cardfox you can set an exam date for a deck, and the app takes it into account so that everything sticks by then.

Frequently asked questions

How many new words should I add per day?

There is no universal number. Start small, for example 10 to 20 new words a day, and watch how many reviews come back over the following week. If daily reviews take longer than you can keep up with, add fewer new words.

How long are the intervals in spaced repetition?

There is no fixed schedule. A new word typically comes back after a day, then after a few days, a week, and later weeks or months. Fixed plans such as day 1, 3, 7 and 14 are a simple approximation; algorithms like FSRS adjust each interval to how well you know the card.

Is FSRS better than the old SM-2 algorithm?

FSRS was designed to schedule reviews more precisely by modeling each card’s difficulty and memory stability, which typically means fewer reviews for the same retention. The method still depends on you reviewing regularly and rating honestly.

Can I do spaced repetition with paper flashcards?

Yes. The classic method is the Leitner box, described by Sebastian Leitner in the 1970s: a card moves to the next compartment when you know it and back to the first when you don’t, and later compartments are reviewed less often. An app simply takes over the scheduling.

What happens if I miss a few days?

Due cards pile up but nothing is lost. Work through the backlog over a few days, reviewing due cards before adding new ones. Some words will need an Again, which is normal.
