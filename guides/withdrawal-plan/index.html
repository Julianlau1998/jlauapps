ETF withdrawal plan: how long will my portfolio last?

Saving into ETFs for years is one half of the plan. The other half starts when you want to live off the money: how much can you take out each month, and how long will it last? A withdrawal plan answers exactly that question.

This guide explains the two basic ways to withdraw, where the well-known 4% rule comes from, why the order of returns matters so much, and what inflation and taxes do to your plan. A worked example with hypothetical numbers shows how quickly small changes add up.

What is a withdrawal plan?

A withdrawal plan is the reverse of a savings plan. Instead of buying ETF shares every month, you sell some every month or year and use the money to live on. The rest stays invested and can keep growing. How long the money lasts depends on four things: the starting capital, the amount you withdraw, the return during the payout years, and whether your withdrawals rise over time.

Fixed amount or fixed percentage

There are two basic approaches, and they behave very differently.

  • Fixed amount: you withdraw the same sum each month, often raised once a year for inflation. Your income is predictable, but the portfolio can run out if returns are weak.
  • Fixed percentage: you withdraw a set share of the current portfolio value, for example 4% a year. The portfolio never fully runs out, but your income rises and falls with the markets.

The 4% rule – where it comes from

The 4% rule goes back to financial planner William Bengen. In 1994 he looked at historical US market data and asked which initial withdrawal rate, raised with inflation every year, would have lasted at least 30 years in every period he studied. His answer was about 4% of the starting portfolio.

In 1998, three professors at Trinity University in Texas published a related study – often called the Trinity study – that calculated success rates for different withdrawal rates and mixes of stocks and bonds over periods of up to 30 years.

In practice: with $500,000, the rule suggests $20,000 in the first year, about $1,667 a month. In later years you adjust that amount for inflation instead of recalculating 4% of the current value. Keep in mind that the rule is based on past US data, mixed stock and bond portfolios and a horizon of about 30 years. It is a rule of thumb, not a guarantee, and fees and taxes are not part of it.

What the example shows

Without any return, $300,000 simply lasts 250 months at $1,200. A constant 4% return more than doubles that. Raising the withdrawal by 2% a year to keep pace with inflation costs about 17 years compared with a flat withdrawal. And going from $1,200 to $1,500 a month shortens the plan by almost seven years.

A useful reference point is the amount you could take out without shrinking the capital much: roughly the return on it. At 4% a year on $300,000, that is about $1,000 a month.

Sequence-of-returns risk

Real markets don't deliver a constant return. And when you are withdrawing money, the order of good and bad years matters. Losses early in retirement hit a larger share of your capital, and the shares you sell at low prices are gone for the recovery.

A hypothetical example: $300,000, $15,000 withdrawn at the start of each year, and five yearly returns of −20%, −10%, +5%, +15% and +20%. Without withdrawals, the order would make no difference. With withdrawals, the portfolio ends at about $217,300 if the bad years come first and about $250,200 if they come last – a gap of more than $32,000 from the same returns.

Inflation

A fixed withdrawal loses purchasing power every year. At 2% inflation, $1,200 today buys only what about $808 buys now after 20 years, and prices roughly double in 35 years. That is why many plans raise the withdrawal every year – and why that increase has such a big effect on how long the money lasts.

Taxes on withdrawals in Germany

If you live in Germany, the following applies in general terms: when you sell ETF shares, only the gain is taxed, not the whole amount you withdraw. Capital gains are subject to a flat 25% tax plus solidarity surcharge and, if applicable, church tax. Equity ETFs benefit from a partial exemption of 30% of the gain, and the saver's allowance of €1,000 per person per year (€2,000 for jointly assessed couples) stays tax-free. The exact amount depends on your purchase prices and personal situation; for a binding answer, ask a tax advisor. Rules in other countries differ.

Frequently asked questions

How long will my money last in retirement?

It depends on capital, withdrawal, return and inflation. In the hypothetical example, $300,000 lasts about 27 years at $1,200 a month rising 2% a year with a constant 4% return.

Is the 4% rule still valid?

It is a rule of thumb based on historical US data over about 30 years. It can be a starting point, but it doesn't guarantee that your money lasts, especially over longer retirements.

Fixed amount or percentage – which is better?

A fixed amount gives predictable income but can run out; a fixed percentage can't fully run out but fluctuates with the markets. Many people combine both ideas.
